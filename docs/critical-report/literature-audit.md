# Literature Audit

## Should I Work Out Today?

This audit locks the final literature set to nine PDFs that the student manually verified through formal academic databases, publisher records or official institutional websites and obtained locally. AI assisted with proposing search terms and organising candidate sources. The final selection and bibliographic checks were completed by the student using ACM Digital Library, PubMed or PubMed Central, ScienceDirect, nist.gov and who.int. Google Scholar was not used as the final source of quality verification, and AI did not replace formal publication records or PDF evidence.

For each item, the full PDF was obtained locally, and bibliographic details and report-relevant passages were checked against the official source and the paper. This statement does not claim that every PDF was read in full from beginning to end. Screenshot evidence of the verification process is retained locally and is not committed to the repository.

## Peer-Reviewed Academic Papers

### 1. Amershi et al. (2019)

- **Complete Harvard reference:** Amershi, S., Weld, D., Vorvoreanu, M., Fourney, A., Nushi, B., Collisson, P., Suh, J., Iqbal, S., Bennett, P.N., Inkpen, K., Teevan, J., Kikin-Gil, R. and Horvitz, E. (2019) ‘Guidelines for Human-AI Interaction’, *Proceedings of the 2019 CHI Conference on Human Factors in Computing Systems*, pp. 1–13.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3290605.3300233
- **Official source used:** ACM Digital Library.
- **Manual database or official-site verification by student:** Yes.
- **Full PDF obtained locally:** Yes.
- **Screenshot evidence retained locally:** Yes.
- **Publication type:** Peer-reviewed academic conference paper.
- **Peer-reviewed:** Yes.
- **Verification statement:** The full PDF was obtained locally, and bibliographic details and report-relevant passages were checked against the official source and the paper.
- **Main relevance:** The paper develops 18 human–AI interaction guidelines through multiple evaluation phases, including 49 design practitioners assessing 20 AI products. It supports communicating capability, making current system behaviour understandable, enabling correction and supporting recovery from error.
- **Claims used in the report:** Visible interpretation, correction, user control and recovery are relevant interaction principles for the prototype.
- **What the source does not prove:** It does not validate this project’s interface or show that the current correction and refusal flows are usable.
- **Risk of overinterpretation:** Applying a guideline is not evidence that a design has achieved transparency, control or safe use.

### 2. Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021)

- **Complete Harvard reference:** Buçinca, Z., Malaya, M.B. and Gajos, K.Z. (2021) ‘To Trust or to Think: Cognitive Forcing Functions Can Reduce Overreliance on AI in AI-assisted Decision-making’, *Proceedings of the ACM on Human-Computer Interaction*, 5(CSCW1), article 188, pp. 1–21.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1145/3449287
- **Official source used:** ACM Digital Library.
- **Manual database or official-site verification by student:** Yes.
- **Full PDF obtained locally:** Yes.
- **Screenshot evidence retained locally:** Yes.
- **Publication type:** Peer-reviewed PACM HCI journal article associated with CSCW.
- **Peer-reviewed:** Yes.
- **Verification statement:** The full PDF was obtained locally, and bibliographic details and report-relevant passages were checked against the official source and the paper.
- **Main relevance:** An experiment with 199 analysed participants compared cognitive forcing designs, simpler explainable-AI approaches and a no-AI baseline. Cognitive forcing reduced overreliance, but the designs that reduced it most also received less favourable subjective ratings.
- **Claims used in the report:** Explanation alone may not prevent acceptance of incorrect AI advice; active confirmation may introduce an effort–acceptability trade-off.
- **What the source does not prove:** It does not prove that this project’s proposed `requires_confirmation` interaction is effective.
- **Risk of overinterpretation:** Findings from a specific online task cannot be transferred directly to exercise or health-related decisions.

### 3. Hardeman et al. (2019)

- **Complete Harvard reference:** Hardeman, W., Houghton, J., Lane, K., Jones, A. and Naughton, F. (2019) ‘A systematic review of just-in-time adaptive interventions (JITAIs) to promote physical activity’, *International Journal of Behavioral Nutrition and Physical Activity*, 16, article 31.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1186/s12966-019-0792-7
- **Official source used:** PubMed Central.
- **Manual database or official-site verification by student:** Yes.
- **Full PDF obtained locally:** Yes.
- **Screenshot evidence retained locally:** Yes.
- **Publication type:** Peer-reviewed journal article; systematic review.
- **Peer-reviewed:** Yes.
- **Verification statement:** The full PDF was obtained locally, and bibliographic details and report-relevant passages were checked against the official source and the paper.
- **Main relevance:** The review screened 2,200 titles, 840 abstracts and 169 full-text papers, then included 19 papers reporting 14 unique JITAIs, including six randomised studies. Behavioural-effect evidence was mixed, no included study was sufficiently powered to detect effects, and feasibility issues included technology, sensor reliability and message timing.
- **Claims used in the report:** Physical-activity JITAI research was early-stage and did not justify assuming behavioural effectiveness or technical feasibility.
- **What the source does not prove:** It does not establish that the present prototype is a JITAI or that it changes physical activity.
- **Risk of overinterpretation:** The mixed, underpowered and heterogeneous evidence cannot support claims of adherence, health or behavioural impact.

### 4. Miller (2019)

- **Complete Harvard reference:** Miller, T. (2019) ‘Explanation in artificial intelligence: Insights from the social sciences’, *Artificial Intelligence*, 267, pp. 1–38.
- **DOI:** https://doi.org/10.1016/j.artint.2018.07.007
- **Official source used:** ScienceDirect.
- **Manual database or official-site verification by student:** Yes.
- **Full PDF obtained locally:** Yes.
- **Screenshot evidence retained locally:** Yes.
- **Publication type:** Peer-reviewed journal article; interdisciplinary review.
- **Peer-reviewed:** Yes.
- **Verification statement:** The full PDF was obtained locally, and bibliographic details and report-relevant passages were checked against the official source and the paper.
- **Main relevance:** The review argues that AI explanation should account for how people generate, select, evaluate and communicate explanations. Explanation is a human–AI interaction issue, not only a model-output problem.
- **Claims used in the report:** Displaying technical details does not automatically create understanding; explanation should be contextual and connected to the user’s question.
- **What the source does not prove:** It does not show that this project’s explanation, similarity or override fields are understandable.
- **Risk of overinterpretation:** Technical visibility can be mistaken for meaningful transparency without evidence from users.

# Project Evidence Boundary

The project is not a complete JITAI. The Figma prototype has no real-time sensors, automatic triggering, longitudinal database or dynamic personalisation. JITAI literature supports the concepts of changing state, decision points and decision rules; it does not validate the present implementation.

The usability study did not measure exercise adherence, health outcomes, psychological health or self-determination-theory constructs. Participants used predetermined Figma outputs rather than a live ML system. The separate ML experiment tested a synthetic text-classification pipeline and did not provide medical diagnosis, screening or professional advice.

Prior literature can justify a research question, design principle or risk boundary. It cannot supply user data, behavioural effects, safety results or product impact that this project did not collect.

# Final Literature Set

The final literature review uses all nine verified sources:

1. Teixeira et al. (2012) and Ntoumanis and Moller (2025) for exercise motivation, autonomy and non-pressuring support.
2. Nahum-Shani et al. (2018) and Hardeman et al. (2019) for changing context, decision rules and the limited evidence base for physical-activity JITAIs.
3. Amershi et al. (2019), Miller (2019) and Buçinca, Malaya and Gajos (2021) for human–AI interaction, explanation, correction and overreliance.
4. WHO (2021) and NIST (2023) as authoritative institutional guidance for ethics, safety and continuous risk management.

No source is included merely to increase the citation count. Shneiderman (2020), Lee and See (2004) and Schembre et al. (2018) remain relevant candidate literature but are not part of the locked final set or final References.
